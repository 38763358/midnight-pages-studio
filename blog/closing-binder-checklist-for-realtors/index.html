What to Put in a Closing Binder

The documents, the home-care pages, and the things to leave out. A closing binder buyers actually keep, and open again.

Midnight Pages Studio · November 7, 2026

A closing binder is the folder your buyer reaches for when they refinance, file taxes, call a roofer or sell the house years from now. Done well, it is also the most useful closing gift you can give: it costs little, it lives on a shelf in their home, and your name is on the spine. Here is what to put in it, in the order people look for things.

Part 1: The documents to keep

Use the final, signed versions from your transaction file, not drafts from email. Not every item applies to every deal; a cash purchase has no loan documents, and forms vary by state.

  • Closing Disclosure or the settlement statement. The CFPB lists it among the four key documents to keep, with the promissory note, the mortgage or deed of trust, and the deed. It also records the purchase price and closing costs the buyer may need for taxes later.
  • Promissory note and mortgage or deed of trust (copies).
  • Deed. The copy signed at closing now; the recorded copy arrives later from the county or title company.
  • Owner’s title insurance policy. Often issued after closing, so leave a slot for it.
  • Executed purchase contract, counter-offers, addenda and amendments, including what conveyed with the house.
  • Disclosures: seller’s property disclosure, lead-based paint disclosure for pre-1978 homes, HOA documents.
  • Inspection report, appraisal, repair agreement and repair receipts.
  • Survey or plat, if one was done.
  • Homeowner’s insurance declarations page.
  • Home warranty contract, if one was purchased.

Part 2: The pages that make it a homeowner’s binder

Documents alone make a file. These pages make it something they open again:

  • A one-page transaction summary: address, closing date, price, lender, title company and the people involved.
  • Emergency shut-offs: where the water main, gas valve and electrical panel are, with space to note it during the final walkthrough.
  • Utility setup list: electric, gas, water, trash, internet, with blanks for account numbers they fill in themselves.
  • A year of home care: a month-by-month maintenance calendar (filters, gutters, smoke alarms, water heater).
  • Appliance and system log: model numbers, install dates, manuals and warranty cards.
  • Trusted contacts: your number, the inspector, a handyman, plumber, electrician and the HOA manager.
  • A “documents to come” page: a checklist for the recorded deed, the title policy and the first escrow statement, so the buyer knows what to watch for.
  • Tax notes: a reminder to ask their tax professional about deductible closing costs and property taxes for the year of purchase.

What to leave out

  • Full account numbers, Social Security numbers and bank details. Don’t print them. Leave blanks the owner fills in.
  • Wire instructions. Old wiring details in a binder can be misused; the transaction is done.
  • Alarm codes and garage codes written on the cover. If you include them, put them on a removable inside page and tell the buyer to change them.
  • A sales pitch. One page about you is enough. The binder earns trust by being useful.

Paper, digital, or both

A physical binder is what sits on the shelf; a scanned copy is what survives a move or a flood. The simplest approach is both: hand over the printed binder at closing and send a shared folder or a USB stick with PDFs of the same documents. Suggest a file-naming pattern like 2026-12-18_Closing-Disclosure.pdf so files sort by date.

Assembling one before a year-end closing

  1. Prepare the template at contract, not at closing. Fill in the address, names and dates while things are calm.
  2. Collect documents as they are signed. Drop each final PDF into a folder named for the client.
  3. Print the homeowner pages the week before closing; print the closing documents after they are signed.
  4. Use a 1.5 or 2 inch binder with tab dividers in the order above, plus a front pocket for loose items like keys’ paperwork.
  5. Hand it over at closing or the key handoff, and walk through the shut-off page together if you are at the house.
  6. Put the closing date in your calendar for a one-year home-anniversary touch.

December closings add one reason to do this well: the buyer will need the Closing Disclosure and property tax figures within weeks, when tax season starts.

Seller binders are shorter

Sellers need a record too, just a thinner one: the settlement statement, the signed purchase contract and disclosures, the mortgage payoff statement and, later, the lien release, plus any tax reporting forms the closing agent provides. A simple folder works. Add a moving-week checklist if they are buying their next home with you.
